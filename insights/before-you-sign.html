Before you sign: six questions every athlete family should ask

A business wants to work with your athlete. An agreement arrives. Here's how to get the full picture before anyone signs.

For most families, the first NIL offer arrives somewhere between practice and a tournament weekend. Suddenly a household fluent in recruiting timelines is discussing content rights, payment conditions, and exclusivity.

You don't need to know every business term to ask a useful question. You do need a process. Start with these six.

1. Does the athlete actually want this?

The opportunity uses the athlete's identity, requires their time, and can affect future choices. Their understanding and preferences belong at the center of the decision. If you haven't already, sit down together and agree on which brands fit, which you'd decline, and how much time the athlete can commit during the season.

2. Do we have the whole document?

Request the complete agreement and every attachment or policy it references. Then check that the written offer reflects what was discussed. Write "not provided" next to anything that's missing. Gaps are information too.

3. What exactly is the work?

"A few posts" isn't a scope. Pin down format, quantity, platforms, dates, who approves content, and how many revisions are included. Check the schedule against classes, training, competition, and recovery before committing.

4. What will we actually keep?

Separate spendable cash from products and services. Then subtract athlete-paid expenses and any representative fee. The result is an estimate of cash before taxes, not a tax calculation. NIL income is generally taxable, so talk to a qualified tax professional.

5. What permission are we giving, and for how long?

Creating a post and permitting a brand to reuse it are two different things. Ask where the athlete's name, image, and content may appear, whether paid advertising is included, whether it can be edited or shared with other businesses, and when that permission ends.

6. What could this stop us from doing later?

Exclusivity can restrict other partnerships. A broad category description can be hard to evaluate, so ask which businesses and activities the restriction covers, and for how long.

Flag any term you can't explain. Words like perpetual, irrevocable, sole discretion, indemnify, or guarantor deserve a plain-language explanation from a qualified attorney, in the context of this specific agreement.

The bottom line

Asking for time to review is a normal, professional request. A brand that won't allow it is telling you something. Get the full offer, understand the full commitment, and bring in the right professionals before anyone signs.
