Beyond the base salary: seven terms every assistant coach should negotiate

Salary is the headline. The fine print is what shapes a career.

Assistant coaches often accept the first number offered. The move is exciting, the timeline is short, and asking questions can feel like a risk. But the terms that matter most in a coaching contract usually aren't the salary. They're the provisions that decide what happens when something changes, and in coaching, something always changes.

Here are seven terms worth understanding, and discussing, before you sign.

1. What happens if the head coach leaves

Many assistant contracts are tied, directly or in practice, to the head coach who hired you. If that coach is fired or takes another job, you may be out too. Ask what the agreement says about a head-coaching change, and whether any guaranteed pay or notice period applies.

2. The length and the guarantee

A "two-year" agreement isn't the same as two guaranteed years. Ask what portion of the term is guaranteed, under what conditions the school can end it early, and what you'd be owed if it does.

3. Buyouts in both directions

Buyout or liquidated-damages clauses can apply to you leaving early as well as to the school letting you go. Know the amount you'd owe to take a better job, how it steps down over time, and whether the obligation to pay you is reduced if you find new work (often called "mitigation").

4. How "for cause" is defined

Termination for cause usually ends the school's payment obligations. Broad or vague definitions give the institution more room. Ask how cause is defined, whether you'd get notice and a chance to respond, and how compliance violations by others on staff are treated.

5. Incentives and supplemental income

Postseason and academic incentives, camps and clinics, media appearances, apparel, and outside income can add up. Ask which are included, how they're calculated, whether they need approval, and whether they're paid if your employment ends mid-year.

6. Resources to do the job

Your success depends on things outside your paycheck: recruiting budget, travel, professional development, staff support, and in some cases a vehicle or a housing or relocation allowance. These are often easier to negotiate than salary.

7. Title and responsibilities

A title such as associate head coach, coordinator, or recruiting coordinator signals your next step to future employers. Responsibilities in writing protect you from scope creep and support your case for a raise or a promotion.

Get it in writing. If a term was promised in conversation, ask for it in the agreement before you rely on it. And have the final document reviewed by a qualified attorney.

Why this matters more for women coaches

Women are nearly two-thirds of full-time assistant coaches, yet they hold 46% of coaching positions on women's college teams and only about 6 in 100 on men's teams.* Terms set early, such as titles, responsibilities, and compensation history, carry into every future negotiation. Getting them right at the assistant level is one of the most practical ways to change the numbers.
